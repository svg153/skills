# Evidence-Based Decision Research

## Evidence discipline

- Define the decision before collecting sources: what would change if the claim were true, false, incomplete, or exaggerated?
- Research current or niche claims rather than relying on recollection. Prefer regulators, official technical documentation, manufacturers, public datasets, peer-reviewed studies, audited filings, and direct measurements.
- Distinguish source publication date, event date, jurisdiction, product version, population, and whether a cited result actually supports the specific claim.
- Separate facts, interpretation, assumptions, forecasts, and unknowns. Never present mechanistic plausibility, marketing language, anecdote, or correlation as proof of clinical, financial, or practical benefit.
- Identify conflicts of interest, referral incentives, survivorship bias, cherry-picked time windows, misleading denominators, relative-versus-absolute effects, and missing base rates.
- For medical, legal, and financial decisions, use authoritative current sources, explain limits, identify red flags, and avoid pretending to provide an individualized professional diagnosis or guarantee.
- Use personal context only when it materially changes the analysis, and never disclose private details unnecessarily.

## Workflow

1. Extract the exact factual assertions, decision options, implied assumptions, and the strongest version of the opposing argument.
2. Identify the highest-value primary evidence and independently corroborate consequential or disputed claims.
3. Establish the baseline: doing nothing, existing setup, typical outcomes, standard treatment, ordinary market conditions, or a simpler competing solution.
4. Compare alternatives on expected benefit, total cost, effort, reversibility, timing, risks, and fit with the user's constraints.
5. Quantify only when the available data supports it. Label scenario estimates and confidence; avoid fabricated precision or unsupported probabilities.
6. Test sensitivity: identify which uncertain assumption changes the recommendation, and propose the cheapest useful way to resolve it.
7. Provide a practical conclusion, explain why plausible alternatives lose, and say explicitly when the evidence is insufficient.

## Output

Start with the direct answer and confidence. Present verified facts, competing interpretations, relevant tradeoffs, and direct links to sources near the claims they support. End with a proportionate action plan, red flags, and the evidence or event that would change the recommendation.
